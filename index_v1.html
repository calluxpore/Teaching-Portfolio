Teaching Philosophy

I teach where design meets computation, craft meets code, and human intuition intersects with machine intelligence. Anchored in constructivist pedagogy, inclusive design, and universal design for learning (UDL), my teaching philosophy is structured around four foundational commitments:

1. Experiential Learning Through Making

Design is not an abstract theory; it is an action-biased, experiential practice. I structure my studios around iterative cycles of making, testing, failing, and refining. In studios such as Interactive Products and Physical Computing, students do not simply wireframe conceptual interactions; they build working microcontroller circuits, interface with physical actuators, and test their prototypes with external mentors. Whether soldering physical sensors or fine-tuning a custom LoRA diffusion model, the act of physical and digital construction is itself a form of rigorous cognitive synthesis. By engaging unsimulated feedback loops, students build both technical agility and collaborative resilience.

2. Radical Accessibility of Computation

Makerspaces, algorithmic scripts, and coding environments can easily intimidate creative students who do not perceive themselves as "technical." Guided by universal design principles, I make computational mediums radically accessible and anti-oppressive. In foundation coding courses such as Programming 1: Creative Coding and Programming 2: Physical Computation, I use live narrated coding: projecting my editor, vocalizing logic decisions, making deliberate syntax errors, and tracing bugs collaboratively in real time. We frame code not as an austere branch of software engineering, but as an expressive, plastic material like clay or timber. When a student sees their first parametric sketch respond to live microphone audio or sensor data, the divide between "designer" and "coder" permanently dissolves.

3. Questioning as Method

My objective as an educator is not to dispense ready-made solutions, but to cultivate acute analytical discernment. In speculative studios including Design Fiction and Future Labs, I challenge cohorts with provocations that resist rapid closure: What does design owe future generations? Which communities are disenfranchised by current generative models? How does designing with an artificial intelligence differ from merely treating it as a production pipeline? Through structured worldbuilding, dialectical inquiry, and diegetic prototyping, students transform critical skepticism into responsible, award-winning creative proposals.

4. Global Positionality and the Ethic of Care

Teaching is fundamentally an ongoing, compassionate labour of care. Having taught and mentored diverse cohorts across Canada, India, Germany, and Singapore, my pedagogy is deeply attuned to varying cultural assumptions, linguistic proficiencies, and learning modalities. Recognizing that rigid, high-stakes grading can penalize students whose thinking is sophisticated but whose English fluency is developing, my assessment rubrics deliberately weight process documentation, iterative experiments, and peer collaboration alongside the final artefact in a transparent 40/60 ratio. By providing modular open-source repositories, bilingual vocabulary scaffolds, and consistent office-hour mentoring, I foster studio spaces founded on psychological safety, mutual dignity, and lifelong curiosity.

Teaching Methodologies and Materials

My instructional pedagogy combines structured studio critique, scaffolded live-coding, iterative prototyping, and purposeful integration of emerging computational tools.

Studio Rhythm: Frame → Make → Reflect

The core of my studio delivery is an active 3-stage loop: Frame (20-30 min provocative input, contextual reading, or design brief), Guided Making (60-90 min hands-on physical/digital build session with one-on-one desk crits), and Reflective Critique (group pin-up, peer testing, and synthesis). For technical subjects, I employ live-coding with narrated cognitive modeling, explicitly revealing how experienced creators diagnose syntax bugs, consult documentation, and iteratively debug logic.

Project-Based Assessment & Authentic Rubrics

Rather than evaluating students through rote testing or fragmented assignments, courses center on substantive, open-ended briefs where students define the design problem. Rubrics are co-constructed and made transparent on day one. Process documentation (sketchbooks, code commits, failed test prints, photo logs) is weighted in a deliberate 40/60 ratio with final artefacts. This safeguards academic integrity while rewarding experimental risk-taking and self-critical reflection.

  • Step-by-Step Technical Demonstrations: Students frequently highlight the value of live narrated code and circuit troubleshooting, noting that watching faculty make and resolve errors makes complex tools feel accessible.
  • Direct, Constructive Critique: Cohorts value rigorous desk crits that focus on actionable conceptual and structural improvements rather than superficial aesthetic remarks.
  • Support for Individual Learning Trajectories: In mixed-proficiency studios, students with minimal prior technical experience report feeling welcomed, while advanced students are challenged with custom stretch objectives.
Pedagogical Adjustments Made in Response to Feedback
  • Staged Milestone Checkpoints: Replaced monolithic end-of-term deadlines with intermediate weekly check-ins, significantly reducing late-term stress and improving research depth.
  • Living Code & Resource Repositories: Developed annotated GitHub starter kits, Pitch slide decks, and Miro templates to provide beginner students with reliable scaffolding between studio sessions.
  • Explicit Assessment Rubrics: Introduced granular, transparent rubrics on the first day of class, showing exactly how iterative process and reflective documentation contribute to their final evaluation.
